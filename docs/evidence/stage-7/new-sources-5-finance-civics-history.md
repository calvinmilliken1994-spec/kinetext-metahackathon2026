## Page styles our components may not serve yet

For the subject-engine design (finance/civics/history):

1. **Multi-year data-table grids.** The IRS release and especially the Fed page (17 per-year tables, 2003–2026, with `...` empty cells, a `75-100` range cell and a `March 4*` footnote marker) are lookup tables, not single formulas. Quiz/compare components can quote individual cells, but nothing renders a year-keyed grid or asks "what was the level on date X / how many basis points changed in year Y" — a table-lookup activity type does not exist.
2. **Multi-item table cells.** CFPB's federal-vs-private table cells each hold four bullet points; the transcription joins them in reading order. A compare-table component with one value per cell cannot represent a multi-point cell without lossy summarisation, and quote checks over such cells match long flattened strings.
3. **Branching/contingent rules with thresholds.** The EC key-dates page: "If no candidate wins at least 270 → the House chooses among the top three, one vote per State; the Senate chooses the VP among the top two; objections need one-fifth of each chamber and only two grounds are acceptable." This is a decision tree with numeric triggers; sequence is linear (same gap R1 flagged for phishing flows).
4. **Question-headed Q&A pages.** Four of the civics/finance pages are literally question → answer (Ask CFPB, "What is the Electoral College?"). A predict-the-answer reveal component could exploit the native structure; today quizzes must re-ask what the page already asks.
5. **Mission-clock / elapsed-time chronologies.** Apollo 11 dates events by elapsed mission time ("102 hours, 45 minutes into the mission", "195 hours, 18 minutes, 35 seconds") alongside calendar dates. The timeline validator grounds `Month D, YYYY`-style dates only; hour-minute mission clocks and durations (`8 days, 3 hours, 18 min, 35 seconds`) have no engine, though they are ideal predict-then-reveal quantities.
6. **Field-list reference blocks.** Apollo's Crew/Payload/Launch/Orbit/Landing sections are `<br>`-separated `Label: value` line groups (transcribed one line each). A matching/labeling component (pair the field with its value, or the astronaut with the role) would serve these; nothing does today.
7. **Statutory citation strings.** The VRA intro carries `[See South Carolina v. Katzenbach, 383 U.S. 301, 327-28 (1966) and Allen v. State Board of Elections, 393 U.S. 544 (1969)]` and `Harper v. Virginia State Board of Elections, 383 U.S. 663 (1966)`. Quote matching survives them, but no component teaches case-citation structure; the embedded years can also pollute date-grounded timelines (a court-decision year beside an event year).
8. **Dated resource cards as content.** Census decennial-census keeps link cards with their own dates ("April 26, 2021", "February 10, 2026", "Running time: 2:05"). Timeline grounding could latch onto these non-narrative dates; Muse needs steering away from card dates as chronology events.
9. **Page-fact errors.** Census apportionment prints "Ratified in 1865, Amendment XIV" (the 14th Amendment was ratified in 1868). Transcription keeps the page as printed (fidelity rule), but a quote-grounded quiz answer of "1865" would be factually wrong — the first stored page where grounding and truth diverge. Teachers must review; consider a checker or curation flag for known page errors.
10. **Served-text quirks.** Census serves AEM debug strings as visible text (removed as chrome); the Fed tables use `...` for empty cells; CFPB en-51 prints "grace period ," with a stray space; investor.gov omits a sentence-final period; the Alaska essay's site banner prints "Purchase of Alaska,1867" without a space while the essay heading has the space (the essay heading was used). Validators must not "repair" any of these and quote matching must survive them.

Served well by existing kit (for balance): numbered processes (impeachment, voter registration, apportionment delivery deadlines), dated chronologies (EC key dates, 19th Amendment, VRA, Alaska, Apollo prelaunch milestones), stated formulas with worked examples (compound interest, Rule of 72, priority value), term-vs-term comparisons (rate vs APR, federal vs private loans, winner-take-all vs district systems), and dollar-figure/percent quiz predict-then-reveal (IRS adjustments, Fed basis points, census counts).
